Oh gosh, where do I even start with my dad. His name is Raymond, Ray to everybody who's known him more than five minutes, and he's turning seventy in September. He's my father and honestly the most stubborn, wonderful, infuriating man I have ever known. I'm buying this song for his seventieth birthday party — we're doing it at the VFW hall out on Route 9 because that's where he plays cards every Thursday with the same four guys he's played cards with since before I was born. They call themselves the Bad Hands Club which is a joke about cards and also about arthritis.

I guess the first thing you need to know about Dad is the workshop. He's been a cabinetmaker for forty-six years, forty of those out of the same shop behind the house, and the whole building smells like sawdust and pipe tobacco and that orange hand cleaner he buys by the gallon. When I was little I'd sit on an overturned five gallon bucket and watch him work and he'd never shoo me out, he'd just hand me sandpaper and say "make yourself useful, kiddo." He still has my height marked in pencil on the doorframe of that shop, every year from age four to eighteen, and he refuses to paint over it. He sharpens every chisel and every plane blade by hand on an oilstone that belonged to HIS father, and he says power sharpeners are for people who don't respect the steel. That's such a Dad sentence. He has this saying, he's said it my whole life — "measure twice, cut once, and don't blame the wood." He says the last part is the important part. He applied it to everything, marriages, jobs, everything. If something went wrong you don't blame the wood, you look at your own measuring.

The thing I keep coming back to when I think about what this song needs to hold is the camper trip. The summer after Mom died — she passed six years ago now, pancreatic cancer, it was fast, four months from diagnosis to gone — Dad didn't talk to practically anybody for about a year. And then one day he calls me up out of nowhere and says "I bought a camper, pack a bag, we're going to see the lake your mother liked." And we drove, just the two of us, all the way up to Lake Michigan, to this little town called Empire where they'd honeymooned in 1979. Eleven hours in that truck. He didn't say much the whole drive. But when we got there he walked down to the water at sunset and he took off his boots and rolled up his pants and just stood in the water, and he said, not really to me, "Well, Linda, I made it back." And then we sat on the tailgate and ate cherry pie out of the tin with two plastic forks because Empire is cherry country and Mom used to buy a pie there every single year they went. We stayed four days. He laughed on that trip for the first time since the funeral. I need something of that in this song but I honestly don't know how much of the sad stuff should be in there, it's his birthday, I don't want him crying in front of the Bad Hands Club, but also that trip is the truest thing I know about him. I go back and forth.

Music, okay, this one's easy. Dad is a Merle Haggard man to his bones. Merle, Waylon, Johnny Cash, and he makes an exception for Creedence because, quote, "Fogerty works for a living." He thinks most modern country is "trucks singing about trucks." He whistles when he works, always, and he's a genuinely good whistler, does the little trills and everything. If the song didn't sound at least a little like something off of a Merle record from 1974 he'd notice, he absolutely would. Nothing slick, nothing with those drum machine things. He likes a fiddle. His voice is low, if the singer could be a lower voice guy that feels right.

Some other things about him, just so you have them. He has hands like baseball mitts, knuckles all swollen from decades of work, and Mom used to call them "the eighth wonder of the world" which he'd wave off but you could tell he loved it. He drinks his coffee from the same chipped white diner mug that says WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDPA which my daughter Sophie gave him as a joke when she was nine and he has used every day since and will not use another mug, he'll wash it by hand before he'll take a clean one out of the cabinet. Every Saturday morning of my entire childhood he took me to the Starlite Diner for pancakes, silver dollar pancakes, and he'd always tell Doris the waitress "one for me and one for my associate." He still goes every Saturday. Doris retired but he goes. He tips like he's apologizing for something.

I should be honest that things weren't always storybook. Dad and I didn't speak for almost two years in my late twenties. I married somebody he didn't trust, and he said so, at the wedding rehearsal of all places, and I told him to stay out of my life and he took me at my word because taking people at their word is the most Raymond thing there is. Turns out he was right about the guy, which somehow made it harder to call him. When the divorce went through, my ex took the dog, took the house, it was ugly. Dad drove four hundred miles with the camper, no phone call, just showed up, and all he said when I opened the door was "I brought the truck. You don't have to talk." He helped me move out and he never once said I told you so. Not once, not ever, not even a look. I don't know if that belongs in a birthday song. Actually — please don't put my divorce in the song, and don't mention Kevin at all, that's a hard no. But the "you don't have to talk" thing, the way he shows up — that's the man. That's the whole man.

Sophie, my daughter, she's sixteen now, she's his shadow. He's teaching her dovetails. She's the only person alive allowed to touch the oilstone. He calls her "Boss" and she calls him "Old Timer" and they text each other pictures of wood grain like other people text memes. When she got her learner's permit he let her drive the truck around the fairground parking lot and I found out later he'd already installed a hand grip on the passenger side, not because he was scared, but because, and I quote, "a man should have something to hold onto when he's being taught patience."

Oh and one more thing that has to be known about Raymond: the whistling contest. Every Fourth of July our county fair has a whistling contest, it's a joke event, kids mostly, and every single year for twenty-two years Dad enters completely seriously, performs "Orange Blossom Special" with full dramatics, and every year he loses to some eight-year-old because the judges think it's funnier that way, and every year he demands a recount and buys the kid who beat him an ice cream. Twenty-two years. He has never won. He has a shelf in the shop labeled FUTURE TROPHY that has been empty for twenty-two years and he dusts it.

I should tell you about the dancing because it's ridiculous. Dad does not dance. Will not. Sixty-some weddings in his life and he holds up walls at every one of them. EXCEPT. If "Amarillo by Morning" comes on — the George Strait song — he will put down whatever is in his hand, find whoever is nearest, and dance the entire song, properly, hand on the back, actual steps, the whole thing. Mom figured this out at my cousin Renee's wedding in 1994 and from then on she'd bribe every DJ at every event twenty dollars to play it. He danced with her every time and acted surprised every time. At her funeral reception, my aunt Carol put it on, and he stood up and danced with Sophie, who was ten, standing on his boots. Nobody in that room was okay. I don't know if that's a birthday song thing or not, I'm just telling you everything.

There's also the whole thing with Earl next door and the tomatoes. Dad and Earl have lived next to each other for thirty-one years and they have a tomato rivalry that has escalated to genuinely deranged levels. Weigh-ins. A trophy they pass back and forth called the Golden Stake which is literally a tomato stake spray-painted gold. One year Earl's beefsteak came in at almost three pounds and Dad didn't speak to him for a week, then showed up at his door with a pie and said "your soil got lucky." They eat breakfast together every Sunday after church and they have never once, in thirty-one years, admitted they are best friends. When Earl had his knees done, Dad quietly staked, watered, and weeded Earl's tomatoes all summer. Then he entered one of Earl's own tomatoes in the fair under Earl's name so he wouldn't miss the season. Earl won. Dad has never told him. I only know because Sophie caught him.

His dog is Biscuit, a yellow lab mix somebody dumped at the end of the road four years ago, skin and bones. Dad said for two weeks straight "I'm not keeping him" while buying progressively more expensive dog food, and now Biscuit has a memory foam bed in the shop, a bandana for church days, and rides shotgun everywhere with his head out the window. Dad talks to him like a coworker. You'll be out there and hear him going "well, Biscuit, that's a bad hinge, what do we think" and Biscuit thumps his tail and Dad says "that's what I thought." When Dad had his knee replaced last spring, Biscuit refused to leave the porch until they brought him home, wouldn't eat. Those two are a unit.

Work stuff, in case it matters for the song: he's technically been retired for five years and he has never once retired. The lumberyard still calls him when a customer needs something nobody else can do, and he still shows up at six a.m. sharp because, his words, "the wood doesn't care that I'm seventy." Last winter he built the whole altar rail for St. Brigid's, wouldn't take a dime for it, and if Father Mike so much as mentions it publicly, Dad changes the subject to the weather. There's a bench he made at the trailhead by the reservoir with a little brass plaque that says LINDA'S VIEW, and he has never once mentioned it to me. I found it on a walk. I sat there and cried for an hour. He knows I know because Sophie told him, and we have both, by mutual unspoken agreement, never brought it up.

He also cannot operate a phone to save his life and I love it. He holds it like it's a live grenade. He answers speakerphone at full volume screaming HELLO like it's 1962 long distance. Sophie set his ringtone to a rooster as a prank two years ago and he genuinely does not know it can be changed, so now the rooster is just, canonically, his phone. Every text he sends is signed "-Dad" including texts to me, a person whose phone has told her it was him for twenty years, and once including a text to a wrong number, who he then apologized to, also signed "-Dad."

One more, and then I'll stop, I promise. The morning of my wedding — the second one, the good one, to Sam — I was a wreck, and Dad knocked on the door of the room where I was getting ready. He was in his suit, which he owns exactly one of. He handed me a little box, and inside was a wooden ring box he'd turned on the lathe out of a piece of the maple tree that used to stand behind the house, the one the storm took in 2011, the one my childhood swing hung from. He'd been saving the wood for eleven years. Waiting for something worth making from it. All he said, because he's Raymond, was "measure twice." I lost it completely. Sam had to redo my makeup. That box sits on my dresser and I would run into a burning building for it.

So yeah. That's my dad. Ray. The song is for the party September 20th, there'll be maybe sixty people at the VFW, his whole card club, the guys from the lumberyard, Sophie's doing the slideshow. I want him to hear it and know it could not possibly be about anyone else on this earth. I want the room to laugh at least once, the whistling thing or the mug or the rooster phone, and I want him to have to take his glasses off and pretend it's sawdust, just for a second, at the part about Mom. And I want "don't blame the wood" in it somewhere, those exact words, it would mean everything.
